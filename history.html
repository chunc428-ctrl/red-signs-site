History of red

5 MIN READ WORDS BY CARINA CHUNG

IN THIS PROJECT

Red carried significance long before modern signs existed. For thousands of years, humans produced red from minerals, plants, insects, and other materials, and some of the strongest reds were expensive or difficult to make. Cinnabar, a naturally occurring mineral that can be processed into the pigment vermilion, has been used across different cultures for painting, decoration, ritual practices, and burial objects. Its intense color made it visually distinctive, while the difficulty and danger involved in obtaining and processing it contributed to its value. In the Roman world, natural vermilion could be extremely expensive. Red therefore entered visual culture as something that was not always ordinary or easily available. Long before a red rectangle meant STOP or DANGER, brilliant red could already make an object feel exceptional.

Cochineal provides another example of the material value of red. The dye is produced from insects native to the Americas and was used by Indigenous peoples before European colonization. After Spanish colonization, cochineal became an important international commodity because of the richness of the crimson it produced. The dye traveled through global trade networks and entered textiles, paintings, and luxury goods. These histories are very different from contemporary road signs, but they show that red has long been connected to material, economics, status, and intensity. The strong red we now produce instantly through industrial pigments and digital screens was once much more difficult to obtain.

Across different cultures and historical periods, red also accumulated symbolic meanings connected to blood, fire, life, death, ceremony, wealth, violence, religion, love, anger, revolution, and power. These meanings sometimes contradict one another, which is important. Red cannot be reduced to a universal equation in which one color automatically produces one meaning. Instead, the surrounding object and situation tell us which interpretation matters. A red flower, a red military uniform, a rewd warning sign, and red blood may share an approximate part of the visible spectrum while communicating completely different things.

Modern safety red developed inside a much more technical problem. As industrialization expanded, cities filled with machines, railroads, electrical systems, factories, automobiles, and new forms of infrastructure. People increasingly needed visual information that could be understood quickly and repeatedly. A worker could not depend on someone verbally explaining every hazardous machine. A driver moving toward an intersection needed to recognize a rule from a distance. A warning had to survive noise, speed, darkness, and distraction.

This encouraged designers, engineers, governments, and standards organizations to create repeatable visual systems. Instead of inventing a new warning at every location, similar colors, shapes, symbols, and words could represent similar kinds of information. The benefit was not only visual order. Repetition reduced the amount of interpretation required from the viewer. The more often someone encountered a particular combination, the easier it became to understand the next one.

The American STOP sign is one of the clearest examples. Today, red seems inseparable from STOP, yet earlier traffic systems experimented with other colors. Yellow was important in early American road signage because it provided useful contrast and visibility with the materials available at the time. Federal traffic-sign history shows that standards evolved gradually rather than appearing fully formed. The familiar white letters on a red STOP sign became standard in the 1950s, when improvements in fade-resistant and reflective materials made durable red signs more practical.

That history matters because it shows that symbolism alone did not create safety red. A red sign had to work physically. It needed to survive sunlight, rain, snow, dirt, temperature changes, and years of exposure. It needed to remain visible under headlights. Its white lettering needed enough contrast against the background to remain readable. If the red faded too far toward pink or white, the difference between the field and the letters weakened. Safety red therefore depended on material science as much as cultural meaning.

The red had to remain red.

Once red became embedded in standardized systems, its meaning became more precise. In American workplace safety regulation, OSHA associates red with danger signs and emergency stopping controls, while yellow is used for caution and certain physical hazards. Inside that system, color becomes a category. It is not simply decoration placed onto the equipment after everything else has been designed. The color is part of the information.

New York’s fire-protection infrastructure provides another example. Exposed standpipe and sprinkler risers are required to use red identification under applicable city rules, while specific valves and connections can use additional colors to distinguish system types. A person unfamiliar with the system may only see a red pipe. Someone trained to work with the infrastructure reads the color as one part of a much larger technical code.

This is particularly interesting to me because I had seen many of these red signs and pipes before understanding what they were for: “STANDPIPE, SPRINKLER, FIRE DEPARTMENT CONNECTION”, and similar terms were already part of the visual landscape of New York long before they became part of my research. The signs were communicating successfully to their intended users whether or not I personally understood them.

At the same time, many of the signs I photograph are not controlled by these official standards. A landlord can install a red “PRIVATE PROPERTY” sign. A trucking company can attach a red NO SMOKING warning. A shop can put a red “NO FOOD OR DRINK” sign in a window. A construction company can purchase a generic red “DANGER” sign. These objects imitate some of the visual habits of standardized warning systems without necessarily being required to use the same technical red.

This creates a cycle. Official systems use red because it has become recognizable for certain kinds of information. Commercial sign manufacturers then use rd because viewers already recognize it. Those commercial signs add even more red warnings to the visual environment, strengthening the association further. The color moves outward from the standardized system and becomes a cultural shortcut.

That is the history I see behind the photographs in this book. Some of the signs have direct connections to regulation, while others simply borrow the visual language created by regulation. They do not all share one origin, but they exist inside the same larger history of red becoming useful whenever a message needs to look different from ordinary information.
